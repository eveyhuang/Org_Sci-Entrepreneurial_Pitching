**Working title:** Measuring Catch and Toss and Its Impact in Entrepreneurial Pitching

**Contribution statement:** We develop a new form of measurement using multimodal large language models (MLLMs) and a concept bottleneck model for studying behavioral interactions in entrepreneurial pitches to address a central open question: How do the dynamics of conversational exchanges between investors and entrepreneurs reveal a pitcher's strategic thinking and sway over investment decisions? Our system provides a transparent and reusable workflow that can capture the interactions in recorded pitches and can be easily applied to other domains and contexts.


## 1. The puzzle

Once an innovator develops a new idea, invention, or product, how do they communicate and convince others of its merits? Organizational scholars have studied entrepreneurial pitching, where entrepreneurs present their ideas to a panel of potential investors, to answer this question. For example, Chen, Yao, and Kotha (2009) show that the preparedness entrepreneurs reveal in their pitch affects investment decisions, and Huang and Pearce (2015) show how investors use “gut feel” to decide whom to back. While this literature establishes that what each side brings to the encounter matters for the investment outcome, it leaves a few critical questions open: (1) Most studies focus on either the entrepreneur or the investor and rarely combine the two in a single analysis (Kalvapalle et al., 2024). Yet whether an investor asks a follow-up question and ultimately invests may depend on how well the entrepreneur answered the previous ones. (2) Even though a small number of studies take into account the interactions between the two sides (Kanze et al., 2018; Chapple et al., 2022), they captured one construct, for example, the framing of the questions and answers (Kanze et al., 2018), and are limited by small sample sizes. This could partly be due to the high cost of having human experts annotate interactions at scale. Whether the many things said or behaviors revealed across a whole exchange move the investor’s decision remains an open question. Answering how a good idea is communicated and recognized in the encounter thus requires a new measurement that follows both parties, across many constructs, throughout the exchange, at scale.

## 2. Core scientific idea (250–300 words)

Our core idea is to leverage the advancements in AI to build a measurement pipeline that can follow both parties, on many constructs, across the whole exchange at scale. This measurement pipeline first uses a **multimodal language model (MLLM)** that reads video, audio, and language together to annotate constructs for both parties. It annotates constructs that existing literature on entrepreneurial pitching has studied, such as positivity and enthusiasm in the pitch (Hu & Ma, 2025), preparedness (Chen et al., 2009; Pollack et al., 2012), and framing and severity of each question asked by investors (Kanze et al., 2018). It then uses a **concept bottleneck model** (Koh et al., 2020) that uses these annotations to predict pitching outcomes. MLLM annotations provide the breadth and scale the question needs, while the model keeps the measurements interpretable and transparent.

We use episodes from Shark Tank, an American entrepreneurship reality TV show, as our main dataset. They are publicly available, and each episode shows the entrepreneurs’ prepared pitch, the interactions between the panel of “sharks” (the investors) and the entrepreneurs, and each shark’s decision to either invest or not. The proposed measurement pipeline allows us to ask: **How do the dynamics of conversational exchanges between entrepreneurs and a panel of investors reveal the entrepreneur’s preparedness and tell us about investor judgments beyond the pitch delivery?**

## 3. What we have done (350–400 words)

**Figure 1. The measurement pipeline.** Panel A: a pitch video is annotated by a multimodal language model under versioned codebooks, producing a record of both parties across the whole exchange; human coders check a random sample; a concept bottleneck model predicts whether any investor offers and reports what each family of concepts adds. Panel B: the record of one pitch as a timeline, turns by speaker and function, each question with its concept and severity, each answer with its ratings, and the decisions marked.

**The measurement pipeline** (Figure 1): We built a measurement pipeline that combines an MLLM and a concept bottleneck model. The pipeline first receives a codebook (Table A1), the same document we would hand a human coder: for each construct, a definition, a scale, rules for applying each code or scale, and examples. Then a commercially available MLLM (Gemini 3.1 Pro) watches the whole Shark Tank episode with that codebook included in its prompt. It returns a structured file with annotations on the venture and each speaker, the pitch, each turn in the Q&A with its timestamp, and the sharks’ decisions. We annotated 100 Shark Tank videos, each 7–15 minutes long, yielding 6,730 turns, 1,516 investor questions, 1,668 answers, and 746 decisions.

For the concept bottleneck model, it pools annotations across all 100 videos as concepts into seven families: venture facts and the asked investment amount; the delivery and preparedness of the pitch; the investors' questions (how many, how hard, how framed, in what order); the entrepreneurs' answers (substance, coherence, context, checkable support, directness, admitted uncertainty); the investors' reactions after answers; and rapport and repair. The outcome is whether any investor makes an offer. The model is a ridge-penalised logistic regression with leave-one-out validation that adds one family at a time, so that we can see what each adds. It reads only what was said before the first investor decided, so no decision leaks into the predictors. It then outputs the probability that the pitch gets an offer.

**Preliminary findings (Figure 2).** Three preliminary results from the 100 episodes answer our research question: the preparedness revealed in the entrepreneurs' answers tells us more about investors’ decisions than that revealed in the pitch; sharks respond to a prepared answer by probing it further; answers and sharks’ reactions to them are the most informative concepts for estimating the possibility of receiving an offer beyond the pitch.

**Figure 2. What the exchange reveals beyond the pitch.** Panel A: the same four preparedness items (substance, coherence, context, checkable support), rated in the prepared pitch and in the answers to the sharks’ questions, for pitches with and without an offer, beside the pitch’s positivity rating and the share of answers that admit uncertainty. Panel B: how many of 100 pairs of one funded and one unfunded pitch each model ranks correctly, with 95% intervals. The baseline model (the venture, the ask, the amount of interaction, and the delivery and preparedness of the pitch) ranks 44. Below it are the baseline plus each family alone and the baseline plus all four families, each labelled with its gain over the baseline and the p-value of that gain. The sample is 100 Shark Tank pitches, 73 with an offer. Intervals and p-values come from resampling whole pitches.

1. In these Shark Tank episodes, the preparedness and positivity revealed in entrepreneurs’ pitches do not distinguish those who get an offer and those who don’t. Preparedness revealed in entrepreneurs' answers to sharks' questions does. Entrepreneurs who received offers gave more substantive (3.0 against 2.7 on a 5-point scale) and more coherent (3.7 against 3.5) answers, more often grounded in a checkable specific (60% against 47%), and hedged less with uncertainty (2.5% against 7.5% of answers) (Figure 2A).
2. When the entrepreneurs answer with checkable specifics, the sharks challenge it less (4% against 8%) and accept it more (14% against 9%). When an answer goes into depth, the sharks ask more follow-up questions in the same line of questioning (about a quarter of a question more for each step up in substance). Funded entrepreneurs give more of both kinds of answers, and their answers are challenged less (2% against 5%) and accepted more (also 14% against 9%). So a well-prepared answer does not end the exchange. Instead, it changes the exchange from testing a claim to exploring and probing it.
3. The baseline model, with only concepts on the delivery and preparedness of the pitch and control variables (the type of venture, the asked investment, and the amount of interaction before the first decision), predicts whether the panel makes an offer worse than a coin flip (44% vs. 50%). Against this baseline, adding concepts on the investors' questions (how many, how hard, how framed) adds 10% (p = 0.19); adding entrepreneurs' answers to sharks' questions adds 23% (p = 0.002); adding the panel's reactions to answers adds 15% (p = 0.052); and adding the rapport and repair between entrepreneurs and sharks adds 13% (p = 0.07). Combining all four groups adds 24% (p = 0.005) beyond the baseline (Figure 2C).

## 5. What we hope to do (350–450 words)

Beyond addressing the risks discussed above, we plan to do three things next. First, further explore the interaction dynamics between entrepreneurs and sharks. The concept bottleneck model tells us which concepts carry information about the offer, but a regression on pitch-level features cannot show nuanced dynamics such as what follows what, who responds to whom, or how the questioning develops over the encounter. The turn record already carries every turn's speaker, function, codes, and time in order, so we will apply the methods organizational process research uses for interaction, such as lag-sequential analysis (Bakeman and Gottman, 1997) and relational event models (Butts, 2008; Pilny et al., 2016).

Second, scale the analysis within and across contexts. Within Shark Tank, we will scale up the analysis to every broadcast season and run the test on pitches we have not looked at. Beyond Shark Tank, we will apply the pipeline to other recorded pitching settings without broadcast or editing, for example, accelerator demo days, to test whether the findings transfer.

Third, build the pipeline into a tool other scholars can use. We will release the codebooks, prompts, saved annotations, analysis code, and notebooks, with a worked example that runs end to end on a small public sample, so that a researcher can (1) reproduce every number in this proposal from the saved annotations without any model calls, (2) inspect the evidence behind any single annotation, and (3) write a codebook for a different kind of encounter, such as hiring panels, grant committees, or earnings calls, and run the same pipeline on their own recordings.

## 6. How organizational science changes if successful (200–250 words)

Organization science has been constrained by a measurement bottleneck. Most studies have relied on human coders to analyze behaviors and interactions. But trained human coders need many hours for each hour of recording, which has kept behavioral studies small and mostly in the lab, and surveys capture what people say they do rather than what they do. Our pipeline turns recordings of real encounters into structured records of who said what to whom, in what order, and how each person responded, with every annotation tied to a definition, a prompt, and a timestamp.

If validated, this changes what the field can study in three ways. First, scale: findings from small samples, such as how investors frame questions to women and men, can be tested across hundreds of encounters. Second, resolution: enacted behavior sits next to perceptions, so an evaluator who accepted an answer is distinguished from one who said nothing. Third, sequence: because order is preserved and decisions are kept apart from discussion, theories of how a judgment forms over an encounter can be tested.

For entrepreneurship, it makes a long-standing question answerable: how innovators communicate and convince others of the merits of their good ideas. Our preliminary results suggest that the question-and-answer exchange between entrepreneurs and sharks can tell us more about the funding outcomes than the pitch. If the exchange is where merit is communicated, it opens new questions for future research such as how a judgment forms over the exchange, when investors shift from testing an answer to exploring it, and whether the exchange matters as much in private investor meetings and in other evaluations of new ideas, from hiring panels to grant committees. All of these can now be studied at scale, on recordings of real encounters, with both parties in view.

### A2. The pipeline

**MLLM annotation** runs in three steps. Running the whole pipeline on 100 episodes took about 250 calls and 15M input tokens, which cost about $120 in total.

1. **Codebooks.** We first define the codebooks as laid out in Table A1. For each construct, we write down definitions and examples based on previous literature, a scale, and rules for applying it.
2. **Annotation.** The pipeline then sends each video to a commercially available MLLM (Gemini 3.1 Pro) using its API with the codebook in the prompt. Each video is annotated twice in independent API calls. This returns a structured JSON file for each video.
3. **Forced ranking of pitch delivery.** Because nearly all the pitch deliveries are rated 4 or 5, we made additional calls to the API to force-rank the prepared pitches against each other in batches of five, with each pitch in three batches, and the rankings are combined into a score per pitch (a Plackett–Luce model).

**The concept bottleneck model.** The model predicts whether any shark makes or joins an offer, based on concepts built from the annotations.

- **From annotations to concepts:** Only annotations of turns before the first decision by any shark are used so that the decision doesn’t leak into the model. Then, the codes of the included turns are summarized quantitatively per episode as counts, shares, and means: for example, the mean substance of the answers, the share of answers grounded in a checkable specific, the share of questions that are hard, etc. This gives 46 quantitative concepts per pitch.
- **Groups of concepts:** The concepts are then grouped by what they describe. For example, the venture and the asked investment amount (6 concepts), the amount of interaction before the first decision (3), the rating and ranking on pitch delivery (2), and the preparedness of the pitch (4). Four groups describe the exchange: the sharks' questions (11 concepts, among them the order of the questioning), the entrepreneurs' answers (8), the sharks' reactions to the answers (9), and rapport and repair (4).
- **Model:** We used a logistic regression with ridge penalty, which shrinks the weights toward zero so that 100 pitches can carry many concepts. Each pitch is predicted by a model fitted on the other 99 (leave-one-out), and the penalty is chosen within those 99. Concepts are standardised; a missing value is replaced by the median of the training pitches and flagged.
- **Comparisons:** The baseline is fitted alone, then with each group of the exchange added on its own, and then with the groups added one at a time in a fixed order (questions, answers, reactions, rapport and repair).
- **Evaluation:** Each model is judged on how often it ranks a funded pitch above an unfunded one (the area under the ROC curve, AUC) and on how much closer its probabilities are to the outcomes than always predicting the offer rate (skill). Uncertainty comes from resampling whole pitches (1,999 draws) and from refitting every model on shuffled outcomes (500 permutations).
