## Note on the statistics

Raw agreement leads each row: the share of units on which the two sides give the identical value, and for an ordinal code also the share within one scale point. Krippendorff's α follows in parentheses because it answers another question, how reliably the categories are told apart beyond chance, and it falls when one value dominates the answers even where the raters mostly agree; the prevalence flag marks those rows. Gwet's AC1 and AC2 and the agreement on each value are in the appendix.

Categorical codes (PR-FRAME, RS-RESP): Krippendorff's alpha (nominal; Krippendorff 2004) follows the raw share in parentheses. When one value dominates, alpha, like kappa, falls even where the raters mostly agree (the prevalence problem: Feinstein & Cicchetti 1990); the row's prevalence flag then points to Gwet's AC1 (Gwet 2008), which the appendix gives with the agreement on each value.

Binary codes (RS-EVID): Krippendorff's alpha (nominal; Krippendorff 2004) follows the raw share in parentheses, and when one value dominates the row's prevalence flag points to Gwet's AC1 (Gwet 2008). The appendix gives AC1, the positive and negative agreement (Cicchetti & Feinstein 1990), which show whether the raters disagree on the yeses or on the noes, and the prevalence and bias indices (Byrt, Bishop & Carlin 1993), which show how uneven the split is and which rater says yes more often.

Ordinal codes (PR-SEV, PREP-SUBS, PREP-COH, PRES-SUBS, PRES-COH, PRES-EVID, pitch_positivity, expressed_enthusiasm, interpersonal_warmth): Exact and within-one agreement lead the row; the appendix gives each beside the value two raters with these same distributions would reach by chance, since on a short scale within-one agreement is high by chance alone. Ordinal alpha (Krippendorff 2004) follows in parentheses and, with Gwet's AC2 with quadratic weights (Gwet 2014) in the appendix, credits near misses. The appendix also gives the mean difference, which shows whether one rater sits higher on the scale, and Spearman's rho, which shows whether the two raters order the units alike.

Clock codes (cut): The first decision agrees when both timestamps fall in the same turn of the reference pass pinned by the coding sheet. A turn includes its start but excludes its end. Missing reference context, times in gaps, and overlapping turns are undetermined and excluded from the same-turn denominator. This rule distinguishes marking the turn onset from marking the decision sentence within it; it was adopted after inspecting the Firefighter1 example. The shares within 5 s and 30 s, median absolute difference, and Bland & Altman (1986) limits of agreement remain timing diagnostics, not the agreement criterion. No alpha is reported. This comparison uses the model's segmentation; it does not independently validate turn boundaries.

Exact codes (venture:ask): The ask is a fact stated in the pitch, so the share of identical answers is reported under the ask's rule (amounts within 1% and the same equity), with no chance correction.

Comparisons (pair:pitch_positivity, pair:expressed_enthusiasm, pair:interpersonal_warmth): A comparison asks which of two clips ranks higher, so agreement with the model's ranking is reported against 0.5 (a coin flip is 0.5) as k of n judgements, with a 95% interval that resamples videos, because pairs share clips (and two coders judge the same pairs), and the exact two-sided binomial p. The appendix gives the exact (Clopper–Pearson) interval beside the resampled one: it treats the judgements as independent, so it is usually narrower, and the p shares that assumption, so it is optimistic. The same figure in thirds of the pairs, ordered by the gap between the two clips' rank scores from the model, shows whether agreement rises where the model sees a larger difference.

Bands follow Landis & Koch (1977): below 0 poor, 0–0.20 slight, 0.21–0.40 fair, 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial, 0.81–1.00 almost perfect, applied to α, the statistic in parentheses; on a prevalence-flagged row the flag gives AC1's or AC2's band. Landis & Koch proposed them for κ-type statistics, and they are used here as descriptive names, not calibrated benchmarks (Gwet 2014 argues for benchmarking agreement coefficients probabilistically). A band describes the value.

The Agreement and α cells of the tables carry a 95% interval from resampling whole videos with replacement (a seeded cluster bootstrap of 1,999 draws), because the units of one video are not independent; so do AC1, AC2, the positive and negative agreement and the modal value's share and the clock's share within 30 s in the appendix. A row of fewer than 5 videos, or a figure undefined in more than 10% of the draws, has none ([—]). Every other figure is a point: the column without unsure units, the figures per stratum and reweighted, the agreement on each value, the modal share per side, the chance values, the indices, the mean difference, ρ and the other seconds figures. A comparison's interval resamples videos too, each pair weighted by how often each of its two videos is drawn; its figures per third of the gaps are counts.

Flags describe a row and change nothing in it. Prevalence: the most common value is at least 75% of the two raters' answers taken together; the flag gives AC1 (AC2 for an ordinal code) with its band, to read rather than α. Small n: a row has fewer than 20 units, counting each pitch, question–answer pair or pair of clips once however many coders judged it; the counts are shown beside the leading share, and a chance-corrected statistic on this few units is unstable. When two coders' judgements are pooled, the units column gives the judgements beside the units, and every figure counts each judgement.

Coder against coder uses the videos exactly two coders judged and the units both gave a definite value. Model pass against pass compares the model's two identical passes (primary and replicate) on the sampled units that have both, each unit once: it is the model's consistency with itself, not agreement with a person. The column without unsure repeats the row's leading figure, as a point, without the units a coder answered with a best guess and ticked not sure ([unsure] in the note): 51 of 640 reviewed rows. Every other figure uses every unit. A unit on which either side gave no value on the code's scale (an empty or missing value) is left out of the row; the appendix counts them. Stage A was rated over the prepared-presentation window the model recorded, which coders were shown; the window itself was not validated.

The exact question concept is not reported: the analysis reads a question's concept only through its frame, so the frame (PR-FRAME) is the concept's row.
